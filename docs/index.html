1) Strategic Doctrine (Non-Negotiable)

Core Thesis (One Sentence)

Western security will fail unless European states reclaim sovereign defense responsibility, abandon multilateral illusions, and leapfrog legacy militaries via next-generation technology—taking operational lessons from Israel rather than EU bureaucratic consensus.

Foundational Beliefs

  • End outsourcing EU security to the US via NATO: NATO should not be a European welfare mechanism; European states must pay, build, field, and fight.
  • Sovereign state realism: security outcomes are state-by-state; multilateral bodies are transaction venues, not guardians.
  • Primary threats: China, Russia, Iran are strategic adversaries; security planning should start with this fact.
  • Business-like defense leadership: maximize the core mission (deterrence + winning conflict), cut adjacent virtue signaling and institutional bloat.
  • Israel as model: vitality, sovereignty, and security competence; treat it as a partner and template, not a pariah.
  • Leapfrog via next-gen tech: bet on AI-enabled command, cyber, space, intelligent systems, and operational analytics and operational analytics and data-centric warfare rather than copying what is already fielded.
  • UN/EU useless for security: neither reliably protects US or distinct European member-state interests.
  • Free speech posture: institutional speech codes degrade security thinking; debate must be protected.
Intentional design: this doctrine is built to provoke legacy EU think tank circuits and NGO networks. Reputational exclusion is accepted, and institutional backlash is treated as an impact signal.

Positioning (What This Is / Is Not)

  • Not “transatlantic consensus maintenance.”
  • Not EU funding ecosystem compatible.
  • Not academic benchmarking for its own sake.
  • Is agenda warfare aimed at elites with decision leverage (capital, media, intel).
  • Is an “execution-first” worldview: strategy is useless without procurement, doctrine, organizational, and training reform.

Competitive Frame

  • Disrupt: GMF, CSIS, RAND Europe.
  • Method: replace their “institutional caution” with memos that are short, quotable, and empirically defensible.

4) Content Doctrine: Anchor in Current Affairs

Public Output (80%)

  • Strategic memos (2–4 pages): the flagship product.
  • Media extracts: one “quotable paragraph” + “hard claim with evidence” per memo.
  • Short rebuttals: when attacked, respond fast with a 1–2 page “correction memo.”

Memo Design Rules (So They Spread)

  • 1-page executive summary (copy-pasteable into journalist notes).
  • 3 claims maximum (each falsifiable).
  • 2–5 concrete policy actions (specific to named states).
  • One chart or table where possible; no academic bloat.
  • One “institutional heresy” line (forces rebuttal).
2 memos/month Quotable Heresy included

Closed Output (20%)

  • Closed-door briefings: 90 minutes, memo-led, no slide decks.
  • Investor/intel “threat briefs”: tailored 2-page addendum for select recipients.
  • Rapid-response calls: 20-minute off-record consults (high-value donors only).

Briefing Design Rules

  • Start with a claim that challenges existing assumptions.
  • Spend 60% on “what to do next” rather than “what is happening.”
  • Close with a short “risk register”: what happens if states do nothing.
Closed briefings are not “events.” They are conversion moments (donors, amplifiers, operators).

9) Appendices (Operational Templates)

A) Memo Template (2–4 Pages)

  1. Title (designed for conflict): e.g., “NATO as Strategic Welfare”
  2. Executive Summary (1 page): 3 claims + 5 bullet actions
  3. Claim 1 (evidence, 2–3 paragraphs)
  4. Claim 2 (evidence, 2–3 paragraphs)
  5. Claim 3 (evidence, 2–3 paragraphs)
  6. Actions (state-by-state: Germany/Poland/Italy etc.)
  7. Hostile FAQ (5 short Q/A)
  8. Sources (tight, reputable)

Preferred evidence sources: official defense documents, credible major press, government releases, reputable institutes. Avoid low-credibility or advocacy-only citations.

B) Closed Briefing Run-of-Show (90 Minutes)

  • 0–10: “The heresy” (one claim that forces attention)
  • 10–35: Evidence + real constraints (procurement, doctrine, politics)
  • 35–60: What to do (actions by actor type: state, investor, operator)
  • 60–80: Q&A (keep it tactical)
  • 80–90: Next steps + conversion (donor/support ask or follow-up session)

C) Attack Response Protocol (So Attacks Help You)

  • Do not argue emotionally on X.
  • Do publish a “Correction Memo” with tighter sourcing and clearer claims.
  • Do send donors a private “Attack Brief” explaining who attacked and why it matters.
  • Do offer journalists background: “Here are the facts they avoided.”
Attacks are not PR crises. They are distribution events.
